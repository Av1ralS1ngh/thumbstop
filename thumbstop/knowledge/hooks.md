A promo is a joke with the punchline withheld: the setup is the wrong and the stakes, the turn is the reveal, and the cut lands on the turn, before any release. Judge only what is inside the clip. A transcript does not show the picture, so read shocks from action lines, sound cues and reaction lines.

## Opening (first 3 seconds)
1. Conflict is the first event, shown as well as said: a slap, a public insult, an accusation, a threat. Greetings, scenery and backstory lose. Feeds are scrolled past in under 2 seconds and often muted, and high-arousal emotions (anger, anxiety, awe) spread far more than low-arousal ones (sadness).
2. A wrong a stranger can judge in one beat: a stronger party wrongs a weaker one, with a visible power gap (kneeling, thrown out, dragged away). Fights between equals and blame that needs history lose. Moral-emotional words spread, and people back an underdog when they feel the unfairness.
3. Sides by role, not by name: archetypes and role nouns (maid, ex-husband, mother-in-law, boss, guard). Viewers must like or dislike someone before they can fear for them or cheer.
4. Emotions that land instantly: outrage at a visible injustice, fear for a sympathetic person, public humiliation (second-hand embarrassment). Shock only works if it turns into a question. Status reversal, revenge and schadenfreude need the wrong shown first. Tenderness and grief are weak openers; romance hooks only when it is charged (a rival, a contract, an ultimatum).

## Middle
5. One guessable question is open by about 5 seconds: it can be said in one sentence, and the clip gives clues but no certainty. Total mystery and an obvious outcome both lose, because curiosity peaks when a viewer is half sure.
6. Show the bomb: the viewer knows something the characters don't (a hidden identity, a plot) while the hero still looks powerless. Showing the hero's power too early kills the underdog appeal. Suspense lasts; surprise lasts seconds.
7. Escalate in threes: two beats set a rising pattern and the third turns it. Lines at the same level, or one point made twice, are the slow middle where viewers swipe away.
8. Stakes are a concrete loss: a job, a child, a marriage, face in front of witnesses, stated specifically. "Everything will change" is weak. Losses weigh more than gains.

## Ending
9. Cut on the turn: the last beat is a new event (a reveal, a threat, an order, an arrival, a blow about to land) and nothing comes after it. Reactions, explanations, apologies, someone giving in, a settled scene, or showing who wins all release the tension and lose. Revealing a secret is fine; showing the outcome is the spoiler. A clip that simply stops in the middle of a conversation, with nothing new in its last beat, is not a cliffhanger, however tense the talk was.
10. Peak last: the final beat is the most intense moment of the clip, ideally ending on the loaded word. A peak in the middle that trails off loses (people remember the peak and the end; length barely counts).
11. The turn re-reads the setup: the reveal reinterprets something already shown (a ring, a name, a document, the opening line). Twists that need facts from outside the clip lose.
12. Cruelty stays watchable: realistic harm to a child, or abuse with no counterforce and no hint of redress, turns outrage into disgust.

## Short-drama practice
13. The shock must be the show's premise. An opening that misleads gets the tap but loses the viewer after it.
14. The strongest promos use dramatic irony: the viewer learns the lead's hidden status, power or evidence while the aggressor does not, then sees the lead mistreated, and the cut comes just before the face-slap.
15. A turn every 15 to 20 seconds (a new insult, a witness, a deadline, a physical act), so a 30 to 60 second promo has 2 to 4 escalations.
16. The payoff is given away once the reversal completes on screen: the identity is stated and the aggressors react, apologise or kneel; the return slap lands and the scene settles; the evidence is shown and the villain is exposed; the romantic question is answered. A small payoff followed at once by a bigger threat is fine. Revenge already completed sells relief, not the next episode.
17. The cut only works if the viewer already cares: an open ending with no clearly wronged party is weak.

## Penalize
- Curse of knowledge: names without roles, references to past events, lines that need a flashback. The writer knows the story; the viewer does not.
- Setup over budget: more than about 3 seconds or two lines before the first conflict, or lines that do not feed the turn.
- Too many people: more than three speaking parties, since each needs a side.
